Obtaining exact output

This section is about using the stabilization methodology to obtain an exact solution. The obvious difficulty is that #math225##tex2html_wrap_inline2225# produces approximation sequences which converge to the exact output of #tex2html_wrap_inline2227# but do not reach the exact output of #tex2html_wrap_inline2229# in a finite number of steps. On the other hand, two types of finiteness occur. The first is supportwise convergence (Theorem~#th:support-conv#583>), whereby coefficients which will converge to zero do so in a finite number of steps16 . The second type of finiteness concerns the execution path. As #math226##tex2html_wrap_inline2235# is executed on successive elements #math227##tex2html_wrap_inline2237#of an approximation sequence #math228##tex2html_wrap_inline2239# for I there comes an N where for #tex2html_wrap_inline2245# the execution path through #math229##tex2html_wrap_inline2247# is the same as the execution path of #tex2html_wrap_inline2249# executed with input I, [#SS##1###]. This execution path is what we have called the <#591#>exact execution path<#591#> in section #faq#592>. As will be seen this leads to an effective technique of obtaining exact output.

Supportwise convergence might be harnessed in order to obtain an exact solution. One would have to use the ``shape'' of elements in the approximation sequence outputs of #math230##tex2html_wrap_inline2253# to obtain an exact solution. At present, we do not have a useful example of this tactic nor a useful example along these lines for the more general ``<#593#>S<#593#> rewriting'', ``algorithms with <#594#>S<#594#> discontinuity'' and ``<#595#>S<#595#> convergence'' mentioned in the previous paragraph.

The fact that the execution path through #math231##tex2html_wrap_inline2255# stabilizes at the exact execution path may be utilized by keeping track of or <#596#>logging<#596#> the execution path. This can be found in [#log##1###]. The <#598#>win<#598#> of the log method is that predicates are not evaluated and one only has to perform evaluation with exact coefficients for computations leading to the <#599#>final<#599#> output. One can dispense with evaluation of <#600#>intermediate<#600#> data which does not enter into the final output. When the intermediate computation utilizes much memory and the the final result is of moderate size, this approach is useful. The computation of Gröbner bases is such an example, see [#log##1###].

Suppose that given a finite set #math232##tex2html_wrap_inline2257#, we want to compute a purely lexicographic Gröbner basis of #math233##tex2html_wrap_inline2259#. In this case, verification whether a candidate G' is really the desired Gröbner basis is based on two matters: one is testing whether G' satisfies the property (definition) of Gröbner bases, and the other is testing equality of #math234##tex2html_wrap_inline2265#. We ran experiments. In one computation of a Gröbner basis in the case where the coefficients are irrational, the approach computed the exact Gröbner basis (in about 100 seconds) whereas a direct exact computation method crashed after the intermediate expressions became too large (with over 216 syntactic elements).

A second experiment involved polynomial division in #math235##tex2html_wrap_inline2271#. Given a polynomial f and a list #math236##tex2html_wrap_inline2275# of polynomials, we want to divide f modulo F with respect to a fixed term order, and compute the list of quotients #math237##tex2html_wrap_inline2281# and the remainder r where #math238##tex2html_wrap_inline2285#. As such an algorithm we refer the reader to the division algorithm in #math239##tex2html_wrap_inline2287# as found in [#CLO##1###]. Polynomial division occurs many times in computational algebra, such as the Gröbner basis computation and the ideal membership test. In this case, verifying whether candidates Q' and r' are the correct answer is simply based on testing the equality #math240##tex2html_wrap_inline2293#. In most cases where the coefficients are complicated such as irrational or transcendental numbers, it was observed that a direct exact computation method took 2 to 3 magnitudes of computer resources and time as was required using the stabilization methodology.
